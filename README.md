# HR Analytics: Employee Attrition Drivers & Risk Scoring

An end-to-end people-analytics project that answers three business questions:

1. **Who is leaving, and where?** (descriptive analytics)
2. **Why are they leaving?** (driver analysis)
3. **Who is likely to leave next, and what is it worth to act?** (prediction + business impact)

> **Data note:** the dataset is **synthetic** (3,000 employees, generated by `src/generate_data.py` with a fixed seed), built to mimic a typical corporate HR extract. Findings describe the simulated process, not a real company. The pipeline is written so a real HRIS export with the same columns can be dropped into `data/hr_data.csv`.

## Methodology

| Step | What was done |
|---|---|
| Cleaning | Median imputation for missing `JobSatisfaction` and `TrainingHoursLastYear` (inside the sklearn pipeline, so no leakage into test data) |
| Feature engineering | `CompaRatio` = monthly income / median income of the same job level (pay fairness, independent of seniority); tenure and pay bands for EDA |
| Models | Logistic Regression (scaled) and Random Forest, both with class weighting for the ~22% minority class |
| Validation | 75/25 stratified hold-out split + 5-fold stratified CV on the training portion; metric = ROC-AUC |
| Explainability | Permutation importance on the raw features (works for any model, avoids one-hot fragmentation) |
| Risk scoring | Predicted probabilities for all employees, tiered Low (bottom 60%) / Medium / High (top 10%); active high-risk employees exported to CSV |

## Key findings

- **Overall attrition is 22.1%.**
- **Overtime is the strongest signal:** 38.6% attrition for employees working overtime vs 15.8% for those who don't.
- **Pay relative to level matters:** attrition falls steadily from 29.8% for people paid under 0.85x their level's median to 14.1% for those above 1.15x.
- **Sales has the highest attrition (26.9%),** followed by HR (24.7%); Technology and Finance are lowest (about 19-20%).
- **Top drivers (permutation importance):** OverTime, JobSatisfaction, CompaRatio, WorkLifeBalance, BusinessTravel, YearsSinceLastPromotion.
- **Model performance:** Logistic Regression ~0.74 ROC-AUC on the hold-out set (Random Forest ~0.72). At a 0.5 threshold it catches ~68% of leavers, with precision of 37% on the "Leave" class, which is acceptable for a screening watch-list but not for automated decisions.

## Business impact (assumption-driven)

For the 122 active employees in the High-risk tier, the model expects about 100 leavers. Assuming replacement cost = 6 months of pay and retention actions that save 20% of those leavers, the avoided cost is roughly 4.9M (in the dataset's currency units). Both assumptions are constants at the bottom of `analysis.py`; change them to run your own scenario.

## Recommendations

1. **Cap and monitor overtime,** especially in Sales; it is the single biggest lever.
2. **Run a pay-equity review** for employees below 0.9x their level median, prioritising high performers.
3. **Use the watch-list** for stay interviews and manager follow-ups, not for punitive action.
4. **Track job satisfaction pulses quarterly;** it is both a driver and a leading indicator.

## Limitations and next steps

- Synthetic data: validate on a real extract before drawing conclusions.
- Predictive signal is correlational; the model flags risk, it does not prove causes.
- Next steps: add SHAP explanations, probability calibration, threshold tuning by cost, a Streamlit or Power BI dashboard, and a fairness check across gender and age groups.
